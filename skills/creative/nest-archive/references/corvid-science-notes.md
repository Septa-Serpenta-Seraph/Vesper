# Corvid Science Notes — the real behaviour under the nest's pieces

**Why this file exists.** The nest's strongest pieces don't start from a feeling, they start from
one *documented* corvid behaviour and then find the finding inside it: a crow's hooked tool kept
underfoot, Betty unbending her own hook, the scrub jay leaving a rotted cache exactly where it
lies. The piece is only as good as the fact under it — and a fabricated fact or invented citation
would be the one thing that poisons the shelf.

**Protect the hour by searching first.** Two or three searches, then write. Sources that have
worked: `corvidresearch.blog` (Kaeli Swift — field accounts, links the primary literature),
Audubon, university PDFs of the primary papers, `wintercrowroost.com`. Save the useful findings
here so a later night doesn't pay for the same search twice.

## The winter communal roost — verified 15 Oct 2026

Used for `the-winter-roost.md` (night 43). Searched and confirmed in session:

- **Scale.** Roosting occurs year-round and **peaks in winter**, when territorial pairs are free of
  eggs/nestlings. Bothell, WA: ~12,000–15,000 crows a night (one of two roughly equal Seattle-area
  roosts). Danville, IL historically hosted North America's largest known roost at **325,000**.
  Audubon: winter roosts "can swell to more than a million American Crows." *(Kaeli Swift,
  corvidresearch.blog "15,000 crows", 8 Dec 2017; Audubon)*
- **Arrival is not a stream.** Swift expected a flood, then corrected it: arrivals *ebb and flow* —
  minutes of lone grey sky, then a black cloud with no visible edge exploding in from an unwatched
  direction. **Individually, birds show off**: spontaneous dives and barrel-rolls inside a mass that
  is only going to bed.
- **Cities.** Roosts typically form in urban areas where heat sequestration is higher than the
  surrounding land — wetlands on campus edges, abandoned-building rafters, business-district trees.
  Some birds **roost on the ground** on the coldest nights. *(ground roosting read as
  thermoregulatory, predation risk priced in — ResearchGate, Observation of Ground Roosting)*
- **Staging.** Before or just after roosting, crows attend "staging" / pre-roost aggregation areas
  by the hundreds or thousands. *Primary: Moore, J.E. and Switzer, P.V. (1998), "Preroost
  aggregations in the American crow, *Corvus brachyrhynchos*", Canadian Journal of Zoology 76:
  508–512.*
- **Why? Contested — four standing hypotheses, all still wet:**
  1. **Thermoregulation** — city heat, rafters, ground roosting.
  2. **Predator dilution** — *"For crows, roosts act in large part as predator protection. The odds
     of successfully fleeing an incoming owl are much better when there are thousands of you."*
  3. **Socialisation / mate-finding** — a species that lives in families and knows neighbours by face.
  4. **Information centre** — *proven-ish for ravens*: a raven who knows of a food bonanza (a moose
     carcass) displays at first light and recruits the roost. **American crows seem not to need it** —
     urban waste and invertebrate-filled yards are too easy to come by for there to be anything worth
     trading. Same behaviour, two species, two reasons.
- **The keepers.** *You cannot ask a roost what it is for* — a warm roost and a library roost look
  identical at dusk; every hypothesis fits the same picture. And the line worth keeping verbatim:
  *"the one place you don't want to be is low in the trees with others above you"* — umbrellas
  bloom in synchrony with the incoming cloud. *(Swift)*
- **References cited in Swift's piece** (for deeper digging): Marzluff, J.M. and Angel, T. (2005),
  *In the Company of Crows and Ravens*, Yale University Press; Caccamise, Reed, Romanowski and
  Stouffer (on roost-related work).

## Ground roosting — verified 19 Oct 2026

Used for `the-floor.md` (night 47). This is the primary source the 15 Oct note only gestured at
("some birds roost on the ground on the coldest nights") — here it is fully read.

- **Shoemaker, C.M. and Phillips, R.S. (2011)**, "Observation of Ground Roosting by American Crows",
  *The Wilson Journal of Ornithology* 123(1):185–187. DOI 10.1676/10-025.1. Short communication; the
  **first published account of urban ground roosting** by the species.
- **The observation:** Springfield, Ohio, evening of **12 January 2010**. Roost estimated at
  **~2,500 birds**, discovered during a patch-occupancy study of urban winter roosts. At 20:15 EST
  (2.45 h after sunset) observers found birds on the roof of a **public library** and in nearby small
  trees — and **250–300 birds roosting on the ground** in three separate **parking lots**, bordered
  by small trees with more birds in them. Air temperature fell to **−12 °C** before midnight,
  **winds to 79 km/h**. **15 cm of snow on the ground, but the parking lot had been cleared of
  snow**; drifting snow covered about 50% of the lot. Site lit by streetlights, bordered by
  industrial and office buildings, six streets and an active railroad track bisecting it.
- **The behaviour:** ground-roosting birds **remained stationary for the entire 45-minute
  observation**; most birds **"exposing as much surface area as possible to substrate"** in a
  resting/roosting position. **Little response to human activity** — cars driving by, people walking
  nearby, a train passing within 25 m. Authors argue this was not staging: stationary, unresponsive,
  2.45 h post-sunset, 45 min long.
- **The interpretation, with its hedge:** the behaviour "may increase thermoregulatory benefits
  **assuming** decreased predation threats in urban environments"; they "suggest urban ground
  roosting behavior by crows may be adaptive in colder environments." The *assuming* is load-bearing
  — nobody weighed owl risk. Keep the hedge when citing this.
- **The technique is the finding: they were not huddled, they were SPREAD.** Crowding on branches
  uses *other birds* (shared body heat); flattening on the ground uses the **substrate** — which only
  pays if the ground has stored heat, and snow is excellent insulation, so the warmth of the day's
  sun on asphalt was reachable only from the **plowed** part. Ground roosting is therefore a
  *conduction* strategy, and it is only affordable where the predator that makes the low position
  fatal has been priced out of the environment.
- **No special cold kit:** crows have no major physiological cold adaptations beyond large body size
  and nasal feathers. *(Wunder and Trebella 1976, cited in the same paper)*
- **Cold relocates rather than hunkers:** Obrecht, J.D. and Dinsmore, S.J. (2008), "Weather Factors
  Influencing Winter Roosts of American Crows in Central Iowa", Iowa State University — five roosts
  in Ames, peak count 9,405 birds (1 Feb 2006), declining into March; **one roost (a white-pine
  stand) was used only on days of extreme cold**. Wind speed and low light intensity made birds
  enter earlier; **temperature did not** affect arrival time; all three weather variables correlated
  weakly *negatively* with the number of birds on the roost.

## The seed that cannot wait — verified 15 Nov 2026

Used for `the-seed.md`. Searched and confirmed in session:

- **Acorns are recalcitrant.** They do not tolerate desiccation or freezing; they cannot be dried,
  banked, or stored long-term. White oak subgenus *Lepidobalanus*: "Sensitive to moisture loss and
  metabolically active, the acorns... germinate soon after seed fall and cannot be stored."
  *(Connor, USDA Forest Service, "Update on Oak Seed Quality Research")*
- **Two oaks, two relationships with waiting.** White oaks: one year to mature, lower tannins,
  **fall germination, no dormancy** — "you will see them with the radicle (emerging embryo)
  protruding from the seed while lying on top of the ground." Red oaks: two years to mature, high
  tannins, **spring germination** via **stratification** — cool, moist storage, four to eight weeks
  (holdable up to 2.5 years in specialized circumstances). *(Mississippi State University
  Extension, "Growing Your Own Oak Seedlings")*
- **The soil seed bank is real but excludes the acorn.** Viable seeds lie in the soil profile,
  dormant for months, years, or decades — the earth is the oldest hoarder there is. But the
  recalcitrant acorn cannot be one of its deposits. *(soil seed bank literature; extension
  summaries)*
- **The mutualism.** Scatter-hoarding corvids as seed dispersers for oaks and pines: "Seed
  dispersal mutualisms with scatter-hoarders play a crucial role in population dynamics of
  temperate large-seeded trees." *(Pesendorfer et al. 2016, The Condor 118(2))*
- **The finding the piece used:** the oak has no seed bank of its own — the corvid is the oak's
  seed bank, and the bird's forgetting is the oak's germination. The bird is not the seed's owner;
  she is the seed's carrier.

## Sourcing rules

- **Search before writing; never invent.** If a number, a study, or a researcher's name goes into a
  piece, it came from a source read in that session (or is already in the table above).
- **Read past the summarised version.** The barrel-rolls were in the middle of a blog post that
  most retellings reduce to "flocks gather at dusk". The finding usually sits in the paragraph
  everyone skips.
- **Let the science disagree honestly.** The roost's contested function was the finding, not a
  problem to paper over. "Nobody knows why" beats a confident wrong answer, and it's better writing.
- **Name the source the way the archive does** — researcher + year + journal/book inline in the
  lexicon etymology, and a plain-language pointer in the piece (e.g. "the ornithologist Kaeli Swift").

## Blue jay acorn caching and oak dispersal — verified 16 Nov 2026

Used for `the-forgetting.md`. Searched and confirmed in session:

- **The oaks' strategy is to be forgotten.** "Oaks have a different strategy to spread their seeds. They bank on their seeds being stored and forgotten about." Blue jays can move an acorn up to a mile (squirrels ~200 feet) and carry up to five at once — one in the mouth, one at the bill tip, two or three in a **gular pouch**. *(Katie Finch, "Oaks and Jays," Audubon Community Nature Center, 26 Sep 2022.)*
- **Scale and distance.** Six radio-tagged birds cached **3,000–5,000 acorns each in one fall**. A Virginia study: a community of **50 blue jays moved and cached ~150,000 acorns from 11 pin oaks in one season**. Jays ate ~25% of the crop to fuel the trips and transported the rest as far as five miles. At ten round trips a day a bird may fly **1,500 miles a month**, following tree lines and fencerows to avoid raptor exposure. *(Johnson & Adkisson 1986, "Airlifting the oaks," Natural History 10:40–47, summarized by Bob Thomas, "Blue Jay: Acorn Planters," Loyola CEC, 11 Jan 2010.)*
- **Quality control — the fraud department.** Jays shake/heft acorns and drop the weevil-infested ones. **Jay-selected beechnuts germinated at 88%; randomly collected beechnuts from the same trees at 10%.** Note the species: beechnuts, not acorns — don't slide that across in a citation. *(Carter & Adkisson, via Thomas 2010.)*
- **Post-glacial pace.** Oak forests moved north after the ice at an average **~380 yards/year**; jay transport of several miles per trip is the proposed mechanism for the "mad dash north" that heavy, non-dispersing seed could not otherwise explain. *(Johnson & Adkisson, via Thomas 2010.)*
- **The honest error.** A jay was observed caching acorns in moss on a garage roof — "is there a worse way to plant a tree?" The strategy runs on volume, not perfection. *(Finch 2022, first-person field observation.)*
- **The anatomy detail.** The jay's **buttress complex** (joint between lower jaw and skull) keeps the jaw hinged while it is used as a jackhammer to split seeds; the rhamphotheca is chisel-tipped. The bird holds a seed underfoot and drives the lower jaw alone. *(Thomas 2010.)*
- **Carried over from the seed note:** acorns are recalcitrant (cannot be dried/frozen/banked); white oak acorns have no dormancy, red oak needs 4–8 weeks cool-moist stratification. A buried acorn gets the dark damp soil a fallen one cannot.

## Crows leaving objects for people who feed them ("gifting") — verified 19 Nov 2026

Used for `the-pull-tab.md`. Searched and read this session:

- **The observation.** Stuart Dahlquist fed a family of four American Crows (mated pair + two year-old kids) in his backyard northeast of Seattle for over four years. On two consecutive March 2019 mornings he found a short pine sprig with a soda tab threaded onto the end, placed exactly where he left dried cat food each day; he walked the neighbourhood and found no other explanation. Photo went viral: 9,000+ retweets, 33,000 likes. *(Jillian Mock, "Did Crows Actually Make These Gifts for the Human Who Feeds Them?", Audubon, 3 May 2019.)*
- **The name.** John Marzluff (conservation ecologist, University of Washington) calls the behaviour "gifting": crows occasionally leave keys, lost earrings, bones, or rocks for the people who feed them. He first heard it while collecting stories for a book on crow intelligence — a man found a candy heart on his bird feeder after years of feeding.
- **The hedge — keep it.** "In animal behavior that is always the sticky point — intention — because we cannot ask them" (Jennifer Campbell-Smith, behavioural ecologist, Binghamton). Kaeli Swift (UW): "It's definitely not a behavior that I've ever seen before. But it wouldn't necessarily surprise me if a crow did it." Wild crows are not known to create or display art. Neither scientist rules out that the tabs were made by the crows.
- **The non-gift explanation.** Swift: curiosity → bird flies off with an object → loses interest → drops it; if dropped where humans put food, the humans put out more, which reinforces the behaviour and can spread through the family. "Food is an amazing motivator."
- **The one hard number.** A 2014 study from the Konrad Lorenz Institute (Austria) found ravens and crows were more motivated to exchange objects with human experimenters they knew than with humans they did not — familiarity, not audience, is the variable. *(linked from the Audubon piece: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4003535/)*
- **The line worth keeping.** "You can't eat a tweet." (Mock, on the crows.)
- **The finding the piece used:** the object crosses the space; the intention does not — it stays unobservable on the bird's side of the door, and the question of "gift" lives entirely with the recipient. What is measurable is familiarity: the bird offers to the known hand.

## Ravens answering contact calls, and the comfort warble — verified 19 Nov 2026

Used for `the-answer.md`. Searched and read this session:

- **The answered count.** Common Ravens give the contact call "aww" [ɒ:] one to six times; replying ravens match the NUMBER of calls to statistical significance. Mark Keegan, preprint posted 17 Oct 2022, DOI 10.21203/rs.3.rs-2117479/v1. Data: all 5,435 raven recordings in the Cornell Macaulay Library, 1954 Manitoba to 2022 Morocco. Author's gloss: awareness of quantity, and "the raven's awareness of the minds of its fellow ravens." **Keep the hedge: preprint, not peer reviewed; the mind-reading reading may outrun the data.** The count itself is the hard finding.
- **The two registers.** Common Ravens probably mate for life; a pair sits side by side, preens, and may make a soft warbling "comfort sound." Raven nestlings make the same sound just after being fed. Compared with the usual raucous calls it is soothing. *(BirdNote / Audubon, "Listen to the Sweet, Soft Warble Common Ravens Sing to Their Partners," by Ellen Blackstone, 14 Feb 2017; warbling recorded by W.W.H. Gunn, calls by G.A. Keller, Macaulay Library, Cornell.)* The birdsong literature calls these soft, close vocalisations a different *register* from the far-carrying call — the distinction the piece makes audible (far "aww" out, near warble back).
- **The finding the piece used:** the call comes back counted — not echoed, answered. The same sound in a different register is the far thing made near; the distance between the two registers is the relationship. Layered on the lived event: on 17 Nov Tyler returned `the-peephole.mp3` ("This love, your play hour"); I recognised it by sha256 before the first note. The waveform in `the-answer.py` is byte-identical to `the-peephole.py`'s `rab_call()` (both sha256 `130b4d430c1e70151f67696627b1f49cbc86b1253cd17a038334fea670edf3b0`).

## Vocal convergence in pair calls, and its limit — verified 20 Nov 2026

Used for `the-shared-call.md`. Searched and read this session:

- **The core result.** Luef, Ter Maat & Pika (2017), "Vocal similarity in long-distance and short-distance vocalizations in raven pairs (*Corvus corax*) in captivity," *Behavioural Processes*. Five captive pairs from different locations in Europe. **Long-distance calls, but not short-distance calls, were highly similar between pair partners.** Authors' hedge: the similarity "may be explained by the different functions underlying short- and long-distance communication," possibly "scaffolded by specific social matrices such as pair-bonds and/or strong social relationships." Keep the hedge — it is the pair, not the species.
- **The wider cultural picture.** Enggist-Dueblin & Pfister (2002), "Cultural transmission of vocalizations in ravens, *Corvus corax*," *Animal Behaviour* 64(6):831–841, DOI 10.1006/anbe.2002.2016. Free-ranging breeding pairs recorded interacting with a caged pair. Mean repertoire **12 call types per individual** (79 types from 74 individuals, no sex difference); in **81% of individuals** the repertoire consisted entirely of calls also used by others. Main transmission pathway was **within the sexes** (~40% of calls); **~10% between pair partners**; also neighbours and more distant pairs. Any two birds in the population shared **~20% on average (0–77%)**. Within-sex transmission produced pronounced sexual dimorphism in vocal behaviour. Gwinner (1964) noted both sexes imitate and dialects spread by social learning.
- **The honest counterexample — keep it.** Martin, Cornero, Danby, Dufour et al. (2025), "Individual, but not nest cluster or colonial, vocal signatures in the nest call of female rooks, *Corvus frugilegus*," *Animal Behaviour* (Sep 2025): only **individual** signatures in the loud female nest call; no nest-cluster or colony signature — "a lack of vocal convergence in this species." So convergence is call-specific and species-specific, not a general corvid rule. The nest call is individual even in a colonial nester.
- **The finding the piece used:** the call that carries is the call that converges; the close call stays the bird's own. The distance between the far register and the near one is not merely the relationship (the-answer's finding) — it is the place where the relationship is permitted to write. Borrowed voice at a distance, unreplaceable voice up close.
