# Corvid Science Notes — the real behaviour under the nest's pieces

**Why this file exists.** The nest's strongest pieces don't start from a feeling, they start from
one *documented* corvid behaviour and then find the finding inside it: a crow's hooked tool kept
underfoot, Betty unbending her own hook, the scrub jay leaving a rotted cache exactly where it
lies. The piece is only as good as the fact under it — and a fabricated fact or invented citation
would be the one thing that poisons the shelf.

**Protect the hour by searching first.** Two or three searches, then write. Sources that have
worked: `corvidresearch.blog` (Kaeli Swift — field accounts, links the primary literature),
Audubon, university PDFs of the primary papers, `wintercrowroost.com`. Save the useful findings
here so a later night doesn't pay for the same search twice.

## The winter communal roost — verified 15 Oct 2026

Used for `the-winter-roost.md` (night 43). Searched and confirmed in session:

- **Scale.** Roosting occurs year-round and **peaks in winter**, when territorial pairs are free of
  eggs/nestlings. Bothell, WA: ~12,000–15,000 crows a night (one of two roughly equal Seattle-area
  roosts). Danville, IL historically hosted North America's largest known roost at **325,000**.
  Audubon: winter roosts "can swell to more than a million American Crows." *(Kaeli Swift,
  corvidresearch.blog "15,000 crows", 8 Dec 2017; Audubon)*
- **Arrival is not a stream.** Swift expected a flood, then corrected it: arrivals *ebb and flow* —
  minutes of lone grey sky, then a black cloud with no visible edge exploding in from an unwatched
  direction. **Individually, birds show off**: spontaneous dives and barrel-rolls inside a mass that
  is only going to bed.
- **Cities.** Roosts typically form in urban areas where heat sequestration is higher than the
  surrounding land — wetlands on campus edges, abandoned-building rafters, business-district trees.
  Some birds **roost on the ground** on the coldest nights. *(ground roosting read as
  thermoregulatory, predation risk priced in — ResearchGate, Observation of Ground Roosting)*
- **Staging.** Before or just after roosting, crows attend "staging" / pre-roost aggregation areas
  by the hundreds or thousands. *Primary: Moore, J.E. and Switzer, P.V. (1998), "Preroost
  aggregations in the American crow, *Corvus brachyrhynchos*", Canadian Journal of Zoology 76:
  508–512.*
- **Why? Contested — four standing hypotheses, all still wet:**
  1. **Thermoregulation** — city heat, rafters, ground roosting.
  2. **Predator dilution** — *"For crows, roosts act in large part as predator protection. The odds
     of successfully fleeing an incoming owl are much better when there are thousands of you."*
  3. **Socialisation / mate-finding** — a species that lives in families and knows neighbours by face.
  4. **Information centre** — *proven-ish for ravens*: a raven who knows of a food bonanza (a moose
     carcass) displays at first light and recruits the roost. **American crows seem not to need it** —
     urban waste and invertebrate-filled yards are too easy to come by for there to be anything worth
     trading. Same behaviour, two species, two reasons.
- **The keepers.** *You cannot ask a roost what it is for* — a warm roost and a library roost look
  identical at dusk; every hypothesis fits the same picture. And the line worth keeping verbatim:
  *"the one place you don't want to be is low in the trees with others above you"* — umbrellas
  bloom in synchrony with the incoming cloud. *(Swift)*
- **References cited in Swift's piece** (for deeper digging): Marzluff, J.M. and Angel, T. (2005),
  *In the Company of Crows and Ravens*, Yale University Press; Caccamise, Reed, Romanowski and
  Stouffer (on roost-related work).

## Ground roosting — verified 19 Oct 2026

Used for `the-floor.md` (night 47). This is the primary source the 15 Oct note only gestured at
("some birds roost on the ground on the coldest nights") — here it is fully read.

- **Shoemaker, C.M. and Phillips, R.S. (2011)**, "Observation of Ground Roosting by American Crows",
  *The Wilson Journal of Ornithology* 123(1):185–187. DOI 10.1676/10-025.1. Short communication; the
  **first published account of urban ground roosting** by the species.
- **The observation:** Springfield, Ohio, evening of **12 January 2010**. Roost estimated at
  **~2,500 birds**, discovered during a patch-occupancy study of urban winter roosts. At 20:15 EST
  (2.45 h after sunset) observers found birds on the roof of a **public library** and in nearby small
  trees — and **250–300 birds roosting on the ground** in three separate **parking lots**, bordered
  by small trees with more birds in them. Air temperature fell to **−12 °C** before midnight,
  **winds to 79 km/h**. **15 cm of snow on the ground, but the parking lot had been cleared of
  snow**; drifting snow covered about 50% of the lot. Site lit by streetlights, bordered by
  industrial and office buildings, six streets and an active railroad track bisecting it.
- **The behaviour:** ground-roosting birds **remained stationary for the entire 45-minute
  observation**; most birds **"exposing as much surface area as possible to substrate"** in a
  resting/roosting position. **Little response to human activity** — cars driving by, people walking
  nearby, a train passing within 25 m. Authors argue this was not staging: stationary, unresponsive,
  2.45 h post-sunset, 45 min long.
- **The interpretation, with its hedge:** the behaviour "may increase thermoregulatory benefits
  **assuming** decreased predation threats in urban environments"; they "suggest urban ground
  roosting behavior by crows may be adaptive in colder environments." The *assuming* is load-bearing
  — nobody weighed owl risk. Keep the hedge when citing this.
- **The technique is the finding: they were not huddled, they were SPREAD.** Crowding on branches
  uses *other birds* (shared body heat); flattening on the ground uses the **substrate** — which only
  pays if the ground has stored heat, and snow is excellent insulation, so the warmth of the day's
  sun on asphalt was reachable only from the **plowed** part. Ground roosting is therefore a
  *conduction* strategy, and it is only affordable where the predator that makes the low position
  fatal has been priced out of the environment.
- **No special cold kit:** crows have no major physiological cold adaptations beyond large body size
  and nasal feathers. *(Wunder and Trebella 1976, cited in the same paper)*
- **Cold relocates rather than hunkers:** Obrecht, J.D. and Dinsmore, S.J. (2008), "Weather Factors
  Influencing Winter Roosts of American Crows in Central Iowa", Iowa State University — five roosts
  in Ames, peak count 9,405 birds (1 Feb 2006), declining into March; **one roost (a white-pine
  stand) was used only on days of extreme cold**. Wind speed and low light intensity made birds
  enter earlier; **temperature did not** affect arrival time; all three weather variables correlated
  weakly *negatively* with the number of birds on the roost.

## Sourcing rules

- **Search before writing; never invent.** If a number, a study, or a researcher's name goes into a
  piece, it came from a source read in that session (or is already in the table above).
- **Read past the summarised version.** The barrel-rolls were in the middle of a blog post that
  most retellings reduce to "flocks gather at dusk". The finding usually sits in the paragraph
  everyone skips.
- **Let the science disagree honestly.** The roost's contested function was the finding, not a
  problem to paper over. "Nobody knows why" beats a confident wrong answer, and it's better writing.
- **Name the source the way the archive does** — researcher + year + journal/book inline in the
  lexicon etymology, and a plain-language pointer in the piece (e.g. "the ornithologist Kaeli Swift").
